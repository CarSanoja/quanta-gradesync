# The problem, in numbers

Every figure here is sourced or derived from a sourced figure. Where a number is
our own arithmetic rather than a published measurement, it says so.

## The marking load

| Fact | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Total working time, full-time teacher | 41 h/week | OECD TALIS 2024, OECD average |
| Of which: marking and correcting student work | **4.6 h/week** | OECD TALIS 2024, OECD average |
| Of which: lesson preparation | 7.4 h/week | OECD TALIS 2024 |
| Of which: administrative work | 3 h/week | OECD TALIS 2024 |

A secondary teacher in Latin America typically carries around **100 students**
across their class groups. Counting summative instruments plus make-up sittings
and formative checks, that is roughly **200 exams a month** to mark. At about
**5 minutes** per handwritten exam against a rubric — reading the working,
applying criteria, writing a comment — the load is **≈ 17–18 hours a month**.

That derivation was done independently of TALIS, and it lands on the same
number: 4.6 h/week is ≈ 18 h/month. Two different routes, one answer.

Over a 10-month school year: **≈ 180 hours**, or **more than 22 working days of
8 hours** spent marking.

## What it costs beyond hours

Marking load is not the only pressure on teachers, but it is one of the
measurable ones, and it sits inside a well-documented pattern:

| Fact | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Basic-education teachers in Latin America reporting extreme exhaustion from overload | **> 60%** | Systematic reviews of burnout in Latin American teachers (SciELO Colombia, 2020; SciELO Venezuela, 2026) |
| Teachers showing burnout signs (emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, low personal accomplishment) | **≈ 37%** | Same reviews |
| Brazilian public-school teachers (n=679) meeting the full burnout profile | **25.77%** | Cited in the same literature |
| Teachers Latin America and the Caribbean must add by 2030 | **3.2 million** (1M primary, 2.2M secondary) | UNESCO regional teacher strategy 2025–2030 |

UNESCO is explicit that most of that 3.2 million is needed to **replace**
teachers leaving the profession, with work overload named among the main causes,
alongside pay and lack of recognition. The regional shortage is concentrated in
secondary education and in subjects requiring specialisation — mathematics and
sciences among them.

The business case is therefore not "save time". It is that a region which
cannot refill its teaching workforce is spending a month of every teacher's
working year on a task a machine can do first-pass, while the teacher keeps the
judgement.

## The second cost: feedback that arrives too late

Marking delay has an effect independent of marking time. Feedback is formative
only while the student still holds the problem in mind; a grade returned two
weeks later is a record, not a lesson. Our measured pipeline returns a cited,
criterion-level grade in **43.9 seconds for a batch of 8 exams** — the change is
not only how many hours a teacher spends, but whether feedback can arrive while
it still changes anything.

## What we do not claim

- These figures describe a workload pattern, not a study of our own users. We
  have not yet measured a real teacher's before/after with this system.
- Marking time varies enormously by subject, instrument and marking style. The
  5-minute figure is a mid-range estimate for a handwritten exam graded against
  a rubric, and the arithmetic is offered so a reader can substitute their own.
- Reducing marking load does not by itself resolve teacher burnout, which the
  same literature attributes to pay, class size, institutional support and
  social status as well as workload.
